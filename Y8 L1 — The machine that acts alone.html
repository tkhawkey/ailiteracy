The machine that acts alone

Students examine agentic AI — what it is, what it can already do, and where human oversight stops being optional — through physical debate and a realistic group scenario.

Before you teach this: what is agentic AI?
Agentic AI is an AI system that acts in the world, rather than just producing text for a person to read and then act on themselves. A chatbot that drafts an email for you is not agentic — you still choose to send it. An AI that has access to your inbox, decides who needs an email, writes it, and sends it without you checking first is agentic. The shift is from "AI as a tool a human directs, step by step" to "AI as an agent that makes and carries out decisions with limited human involvement." You don't need deep technical knowledge to teach this — one clear example, given to you in the Live AI Demo phase below, is enough.
Rationale
Intreoduces the idea of agentic AI and gets students thinking about the possible benefits and consequences of agentic AI.
HookHuman oversight — where's the line?0–10 min

Students stand in the middle of the room. Tell them that one end of the room represents the opinion "Humans should have oversight" and the other end of the room represents the opinion "AI can handle this completely alone". Explain this is a continuum, they could place themselves anywhere along the imaginary line. Read each of the 5 statements. After each one, they physically move to show their opinion.

  • An AI automatically sends apology emails when a flight is cancelled.
  • An AI analyses medical scans and flags possible tumours — without telling the radiologist first.
  • An AI adjusts a bridge's structural supports based on sensor data during a storm.
  • An AI books a holiday for you based on your preferences — including your passport and payment details.
  • An AI grades student essays and enters marks in the school system without teacher review.

After each move, ask 2–3 students to justify their position.

"You've just made five decisions about where humans need to stay in control. You didn't all agree. Let's look at why and what kind of AI we're actually talking about."
Live AI DemoAgentic AI in action10–17 min

Open Copilot. Type: "Plan the end-of-year school event. Make it good."

Let the response generate. Then ask: "What did the AI decide? What assumptions did it make? What did it not ask us?"

Now explain the upgrade: "Imagine that same AI had access to the school calendar, the budget system, the venue booking system, and your email. It doesn't just plan — it acts. It books the venue, orders the catering, invites the guests, pays for everything. By the time you check your phone, the event is organised."

Introduce agentic AI: AI that doesn't just generate text — it takes actions in the world on your behalf, often in a sequence of steps, with access to real systems.

Teacher note

Current examples students may know: AI scheduling assistants, AI coding agents, AI shopping tools. The category is expanding rapidly. You don't need to be exhaustive — one concrete example is enough.

ActivityWritten follow-up, then group scenario17–42 min

Distribute the student resource sheet. Ask students to complete Part 1 individually (5–7 min): five new scenarios — different from the opening ones — each with a scale to mark their position and space to write a sentence explaining their reasoning.

Then move to Part 2. Groups of 4–5 read the scenario: a school AI system has access to every student's essay folder. It flags any student whose writing has declined compared to their previous work, and automatically emails their parents when a student is flagged. Teachers only see the flagged students' work — unflagged work is never reviewed.

Groups discuss four questions (printed on the sheet):

  • What could go wrong — even if the AI is technically accurate?
  • At what point should a human have been involved?
  • Who is responsible if the AI's assessment is wrong?
  • Would you want this system operating on your work?

Groups share their most important finding (90 seconds each). Teacher tracks on the board: what changes when AI acts, not just advises?

Teacher note

Push students beyond 'privacy is bad'. The more interesting question is accountability. If AI makes a decision and it's wrong, who apologises? Who fixes it? AI systems cannot currently be held responsible in the way humans can.

PlenaryWhat changes the stakes?42–50 min

Tell students directly: here are four factors that make agentic AI higher stakes. Display them on the board with their one-line definitions:

  • Reversibility — can the action be undone?
  • Accountability — is a human clearly responsible for the outcome?
  • Transparency — does anyone know what the AI decided and why?
  • Consent — did the people affected agree to AI involvement?

Then lead a discussion applying them. Return to the Part 2 scenario first — for each factor, ask which one is missing or weak in the parent-emailing AI, and why that matters. Then return to the opening spectrum: "Would you move for any of the five scenarios now? Which factor changed your mind?"

"Agentic AI isn't better or worse than AI you just talk to. It's different — because it acts. And when something acts in the world, the question of who's responsible becomes important."
