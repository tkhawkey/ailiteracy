Year 6 Lesson 2 50 min AI & the world

Who made this — and who owns it?

Students interrogate the ethics of AI-generated creative work — who it benefits, who it harms, and what 'ownership' even means when a machine learned from millions of human creators.

Cambridge Digital Literacy objectives
5DW.04Understand that when AI generates new content, it is based on training data and this data use has ethical impacts on creative professionals, such as authors and scientists.
6DW.01Understand how digital technology, including AI, can be disruptive and that there are benefits and risks associated with this disruption.
6DW.06Know that AI-generated content may breach copyright or integrity rules.

Rationale
Positions copyright and training data as a values question, not a legal one. The structured debate ensures every student has to articulate and defend a position — not just observe one.
Live AI DemoBeautiful — but who made it?0–8 min

Five AI-generated artworks are in the lesson folder. Open and display them one at a time — or generate one live if you prefer the immediacy. Either works; the reveal lands the same way.

If displaying from folder: open the PNGs in order — Dalí, Frida Kahlo, Kandinsky, Basquiat, Hokusai. Before each one, show a quick Google Image search of the real artist's work so students can see the style being mimicked.

If generating live (stronger opening — students watch it appear): choose one prompt below, show the real artwork first, then type the prompt and let the image generate without commentary.

Artist Show first Copilot prompt
Dalí The Persistence of Memory (1931) — search "Dalí melting clocks" "Make me an image in the style of Salvador Dalí showing Bratislava Castle slowly melting into a vast surreal desert, with stretched clocks draped over the battlements and a blood-red sky behind."
Frida Kahlo Self-Portrait with Thorn Necklace (1940) — search "Frida Kahlo self portrait flowers" "Make me an image in the style of Frida Kahlo showing a formal self-portrait of a young girl with dark braided hair, surrounded by Slovak wildflowers and folk embroidery patterns, with a direct unflinching gaze."
Kandinsky Composition VIII (1923) — search "Kandinsky Composition VIII Guggenheim" "Make me an image in the style of Wassily Kandinsky showing the sound of a full school orchestra visualised as exploding abstract shapes, bold primary colours and sharp geometric lines radiating outward from the centre."
Basquiat Untitled (Skull) (1981) — search "Basquiat skull 1981 painting" "Make me an image in the style of Jean-Michel Basquiat showing a large human head covered entirely in layered graffiti tags, scrawled words, crude anatomical diagrams and bold black outlines on a raw textured background."
Hokusai The Great Wave off Kanagawa (1831) — search "Hokusai great wave" "Make me an image in the style of Hokusai's woodblock prints showing a towering foamy wave crashing over the Danube river, with the jagged peaks of the Slovak mountains visible in the background and tiny boats struggling in the surge."

After students have seen 2–3 images: reveal they were all made by AI in seconds from a text prompt. Watch the room shift. Ask: "Does knowing that change how you feel about them?" Take a few responses — don't adjudicate. Note the disagreement.

TeachHow AI learned to make art8–20 min

Explain training data with a simple analogy: "Imagine you studied every painting ever made — millions of them — and got so good at patterns that you could produce something that looked like any style, any combination. That's what AI image models do. Except the 'studying' involved copying millions of images without asking the artists."

Introduce three connected ideas:

  • Training data — the human-made content AI learned from.
  • Copyright — the legal right artists have over their work.
  • The question — if AI learns from your work and then competes with you, is that fair?

Show a brief contrast: a real artist's portfolio vs. an AI tool generating their exact style on demand. No need to resolve — just make the tension visible.

Teacher note

Students often ask 'is this illegal?' The honest answer is: it depends on the country and the case — courts are still deciding. Redirect to the ethics question: even if it's legal, is it right?

Activity — Group PrepPrepare your position20–28 min

Assign groups a role card: Artist, AI Company, Everyday User, Young Creator, or Jury. Groups of 3–4. Read the card together and prepare a 60-second opening statement.

The Jury group prepares three questions to ask the other groups.

Teacher note

Circulate during prep. Groups often struggle to argue a position they personally disagree with — that's the point. Prompt: 'Your character believes this. What's the strongest argument they can make?'

Activity — The DebateOpening statements + open debate28–45 min

Round 1 (15 min): Each non-Jury group delivers their 60-second opening statement. Jury listens and takes notes. No interruptions during opening statements.

Round 2 (7 min): Open debate. Jury poses their prepared questions. Groups respond. Teacher moderates — ensure all voices are heard, not just the loudest.

Key moderator moves:

  • Direct quiet groups: 'I haven't heard from the Everyday User yet.'
  • Push unsupported claims: 'That's an interesting position — can you say why?'
  • Surface tension: 'Does the Artist group want to respond to that?'
PlenaryThe Jury verdict45–50 min

Jury group delivers a 60-second verdict: which argument was most convincing, and why? They don't have to agree with the argument — just assess its strength.

Teacher closes:

"There is no right answer here yet — not legally, not ethically. What you've done today is think carefully about a question that adults, courts, and governments are still arguing about. The question isn't going away. And you're the first generation who will have to answer it — as creators, as users, and as citizens."
