Year 7 Lesson 2 50 min Safety & wellbeing

Your data, their model

Students trace what happens to the data they send to AI systems, sort prompts by privacy risk, and write their own three AI-use rules — grounded in how the technology actually works.

Cambridge Digital Literacy objectives
7SW.04Understand that AI use contributes to a digital footprint that may be stored and used to train AI systems.
7DW.05Evaluate the use by AI tools and digital service providers of other people's content and metadata.
Resources
  • Projector + AI tool (Copilot)
  • Y7 L2 — Student resource to print.html — Prompt Sort cards + My Three AI Rules sheet, 1 per student
  • Y7 L2 — Student resource to print.html
Rationale
Asks what happens to student data as a question of agency, not a scare story. The Prompt Sort makes invisible data flows visible and discussable without catastrophising.
HookThe unsubmitted prompt0–8 min

Open Copilot on the projector. Begin typing slowly — let the class read along:

"I feel really embarrassed about something that happened at school today and I don't know who to talk to about it..."

Stop. Don't press send. Ask: "If I sent this — what travels? Where does it go? Who could read it?"

Take 3–4 answers. Students usually say 'the company', 'the internet', 'maybe hackers'. All partially right. Hold the full answer.

"Before I show you what actually happens, I want to look at what kinds of things people type into AI — and think about which ones carry risk."
ActivityPrompt Sort8–22 min

Distribute the Prompt Sort sheet. Students work in pairs to sort 16 prompts into three columns: Safe / Maybe — depends / Avoid.

After 8 minutes, compare with another pair. Where did you disagree? Why?

Brief class discussion — pick 3–4 disputed prompts and examine them. Key questions: "What would a company know about you from this prompt? Could it identify you? Could it embarrass you?"

Teacher note

Students often argue that personal prompts are fine because 'it's anonymous'. Push back: AI companies log IP addresses, account details, and session metadata. Truly anonymous AI use is rare.

TeachWhere your data goes22–30 min

Draw the data flow on the board: You type → Sent to company servers → Processed → Response returned → Log retained → (possibly) Used in training.

Explain: most commercial AI tools retain conversation logs. Some use them to improve the model. Even 'private' mode often means 'not shown to other users' — not 'deleted'.

Return to the unsubmitted prompt from the Hook. Walk through where it would travel if sent. Ask: "Feeling different about it now?"

Teacher note

This varies by tool and varies over time as policies change. If students ask about a specific app, direct them to its privacy policy — and praise the instinct to check.

DiscussionWhat should you share?30–42 min

Present three cases and discuss each briefly:

  • Case 1: A student types their full name, school, and a problem they're having with a friend into an AI chatbot. What's the risk?
  • Case 2: A student uses AI to help revise for an exam — factual questions, no personal information. What's the risk?
  • Case 3: A student confides in a companion chatbot about family difficulties. What's the risk?

For Case 3, introduce: "This is where data privacy and emotional wellbeing intersect. The risk isn't just who reads the data — it's also what the relationship costs you if it replaces real support."

PlenaryMy Three AI Rules42–50 min

Students complete the 'My Three AI Rules' section of the sheet individually: three rules they'll personally apply when using AI, grounded in what they've learned today.

Take 5–6 aloud. Common strong responses include: never type full name + school, don't share things you wouldn't share with a teacher, assume everything you type could be read.

"You don't need to be afraid of AI tools. You just need to be thoughtful about what you give them. Because what you give them isn't just a prompt — it's data. And data has a longer life than a conversation."
Links forward

Key idea

When you type into an AI, those words don’t disappear — they become data, and that data belongs to someone else.
