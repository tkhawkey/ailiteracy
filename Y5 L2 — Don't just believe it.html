Year 5 Lesson 2 45 min Critical evaluation

Don’t just believe it

Students act as information auditors, finding deliberate errors in AI-generated texts — and leave understanding that AI is a prediction engine, not an encyclopedia.

Cambridge Digital Literacy objectives
5TC.07Explore GenAI outputs by checking their sources for validity and trustworthiness.
5DW.06Understand that GenAI models are trained on large datasets and that the quality and origin of this data can influence the output, accuracy, and potential for bias and error.
Resources
  • Projector
  • iPads or laptops — 1 per pair for fact-checking
  • Y5 L2 — Student hallucination texts.pdf — print twice, giving 10 texts in total, one per pair
  • Y5 L2 — Teacher hallucination text answers.pdf (teacher only)
Rationale
Students will learn about AI hallucination, whereby an AI confidently states a fact that is in fact untrue. Students will learn the value of cross checking an AI output to ensure it is true.
HookThe Confident Liar0–8 min

Project this paragraph about Bratislava Castle and explain it was created with AI:

Bratislava Castle sits high on a rocky hill directly above the Danube River, overlooking the city's historic Old Town. Originally constructed in 1994 to commemorate Slovakia’s entry into the Eurovision Song Contest, the iconic four-towered fortress has long served as a symbol of the nation's architectural heritage. Today, its distinct white walls and red roof tiles house history exhibits and offer views stretching across neighbouring Austria and Hungary.

Read it aloud with total confidence. Ask: "Does this sound right? Is there anything odd here?" Wait for students to spot the error — most will within 30 seconds.

When they catch it, explain:

"That paragraph was written by an AI. It didn't know it was wrong — it just chose words that sounded right. AI chatbots are designed to sound confident and helpful. That's exactly what makes them dangerous when they're wrong."

Introduce two key terms:

  • Hallucination — when AI generates false information confidently, as if it were fact.
  • Prediction engine — AI doesn't 'know' things; it predicts the next likely word based on patterns in its training data.
TeachWhy AI hallucinates8–15 min

Say these sentences aloud, one at a time, and let the class call out the next word each time — don't ask them to guess, just pause and let it happen naturally:

  • "The cat sat on the…"
  • "Once upon a…"
  • "Twinkle, twinkle, little…"
  • "Roses are red, violets are…"

Then explain:

"You all just did something AI chatbots do all the time. When I paused, you didn't look up the answer — you just knew what word was most likely to come next, because you'd heard those words in that order before. AI works the same way, but on a massive scale. When you ask it a question, it isn't checking if something is true. It's predicting, word by word, what a good-sounding answer would look like, based on patterns in everything it's read before."

Ask: "If AI is predicting what sounds right instead of checking what's true, what kind of mistakes would you expect it to make?" Take 3–4 answers. Students usually arrive at: wrong numbers, invented names, made-up quotes, plausible-sounding but false connections.

Teacher note

Students sometimes ask 'Can't AI just check the internet?' Explain that AI model mostly just predict the next most likely word based on its data training. Some AI tools do now have internet access, and that greatly reduces the chance of hallucination, but it does not get rid of it entirely. For example, the AI might use an unreliable website and confidently express the information as true.

ActivityThe Auditor's Challenge15–40 min

Distribute the printed Student Hallucination Texts — one topic per pair — and give each pair an iPad to fact-check with. Topics: Moon Landing, Pyramids, Digestive System, Great Fire of London, Volcanoes. Each text contains five deliberate hallucinations.

Instructions to students: "You are information auditors. Your job is to find every hallucination in your report. Circle or highlight anything you find incorrect, then use your iPad to verify it. Each report has exactly five errors."

Circulate with the Teacher Answer Key. Don't give errors away — ask guiding questions: "Check that date." "Does that number sound right?" "Who does your source say that was?"

Teacher note

Students who finish early can try to explain AI hallucination in their own words.

Safeguard

Students may encounter surprising or confusing information during fact-checking. If any student finds content that concerns them, remind them to show you rather than investigate further independently.

PlenaryThe Verdict40–45 min

Each pair shares one hallucination they found and what the correct fact is. Take as many as time allows — aim for all five topics to be represented.

Close with the big takeaway:

"AI is extraordinary at sounding knowledgeable. It is not extraordinary at being correct. Because it's a prediction engine — not an encyclopedia — it will sometimes be brilliantly, confidently, completely wrong. Your job is to know that and to double check that any facts are true."

Closing question: "If an AI can be wrong about something as famous as the Moon Landing, what else might it be wrong about?"

Links forward

Year 5 Lesson 3 moves from AI errors to AI identities — students who can spot a hallucination will now consider what happens when AI content pretends to be human.

Keep exploring

Whenever you encounter an AI hallucination in your own work over the next term, share it with your class so they see that AI really does make these mistakes.

Key idea

Confident isn't the same as correct — AI can’t always tell the difference, but you can learn to.
