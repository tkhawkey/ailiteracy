Year 7 Lesson 3 50 min AI & the world

The cost of clever

Students investigate the hidden energy and environmental costs of AI — leaving with a felt understanding that using a powerful tool responsibly starts with knowing what it actually costs.

Cambridge Digital Literacy objectives
7DW.02Understand the environmental impact of digital systems, including the energy and water consumption of data centres and AI infrastructure.
7SW.04Consider the ethical responsibility of individuals and organisations when using AI tools at scale.

HookWhich costs more?0–8 min

No context given. Just say: "I'm going to give you three pairs. For each pair, vote: which one is worse for the environment?" Students stand for A, sit for B, or stay where they are if unsure.

Pair 1: (A) Boiling a kettle once OR (B) One second of a global data centre running at capacity?

Pair 2: (A) A ten-minute shower OR (B) Asking an AI to generate ten images?

Pair 3: (A) A return flight from London to New York OR (B) Training a large language model like GPT-4 once — which cost more?

Reveal: the data centre wins Pair 1 (thousands of kettles, running constantly). GPT image generation is surprisingly energy-hungry — Pair 2 is debatable but closer than most expect. Pair 3: GPT-4's training has been estimated at carbon equivalent to over 500 transatlantic return flights.

"You've used AI tools this year. Let's find out what that actually cost — and I don't mean money."
TeachWhere does the power go?8–20 min

What is a data centre? A building — or a campus of buildings — filled with servers: computers that never switch off, answering millions of requests per second, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year. There are thousands of these globally. They don't look dramatic. They look like warehouses. But they consume more electricity than many countries.

Two kinds of AI energy cost:

  • Training — building the model from scratch. Done once (or a few times for major versions). Extremely expensive. Training GPT-3 emitted an estimated 552 tonnes of CO₂ — roughly equivalent to 60 London–New York return flights. More capable models cost proportionally more.
  • Inference — actually answering your questions. Done billions of times per day. Each individual query costs less than training, but the scale means it adds up. A ChatGPT query uses roughly 10× more energy than a standard Google search.

Water: Servers run hot. Cooling them requires enormous volumes of water — either through evaporative cooling towers or liquid cooling systems. Microsoft's Iowa data centre reportedly used around 6 million litres of water per day during summer months. In drought-prone regions, this creates direct competition with agriculture and drinking water.

Where does the power come from? Tech companies increasingly invest in renewable energy — but demand is growing faster than clean supply in most regions. When a data centre draws from the grid at peak times, it's often pulling fossil-fuel-generated power. The "carbon-neutral" claims of major AI companies are contested by researchers.

Teacher note

The numbers here are order-of-magnitude estimates, not precise figures. They vary significantly by model, hardware generation, and energy source. What matters is the scale — AI infrastructure is a major and growing consumer of energy and water, and this is rarely discussed when AI is promoted in schools or in the media.

Live AI DemoAsk the AI about itself20–27 min

Open Copilot. Ask it two questions in sequence:

Question 1: "How much energy and water does it take to train a large language model like yourself, and what's the environmental impact?"

Let it answer. It should give a reasonably honest account — AI companies have improved their disclosure, though gaps remain.

Question 2: "Is it your responsibility — or the company that built you — to tell users about this environmental cost? Or is it the user's responsibility to find out?"

This second question doesn't have a right answer. The point is the shape of the argument. Take 3–4 student responses before moving on.

"Who should be responsible for telling people this? The AI? The company? Governments? Nobody?"

Teacher note

AI tools are generally willing to discuss their own environmental footprint when asked. This can be a useful moment: the tool you're using to ask the question is itself part of the issue. That reflexivity is worth naming.

ActivityWorth the cost?27–42 min

Distribute the carbon comparison cards (one per group). Each card lists six AI use cases with an approximate energy-cost band: Low / Medium / High / Very high.

The six scenarios:

  • Asking an AI a factual question (one query) — Low
  • Generating a set of ten AI images — Medium
  • Using AI to translate a 10-page document — Low–Medium
  • Training a custom AI model on a company's internal data — Very high
  • Running a personalised AI tutor for one student, daily, for a full school year — Medium–High
  • An AI system monitoring and optimising a city's traffic lights, 24/7 — Very high

Groups plot each scenario on a 2×2 matrix:

  • X-axis: Environmental cost (Low → High)
  • Y-axis: Social or educational value (Low → High)

The interesting quadrant is top-right: high cost, high value. These are the genuinely hard decisions. Groups discuss: "Is this worth it? Who should decide? And what would 'worth it' even mean?"

Each group shares their most contested placement. Aim for the traffic-monitoring and AI-tutor scenarios — these generate the most interesting disagreement.

Teacher note

There are no correct placements. The value of different AI uses is genuinely contested. Encourage groups to explain their reasoning rather than defend a position — the goal is to notice that these decisions have a cost that is usually invisible.

DiscussionWho should decide?42–48 min

Quick whole-class discussion — three questions, 2 minutes each:

  • "Should AI products carry a carbon label, the way food carries calorie information? Why or why not?"
  • "Is it the user's responsibility to use AI less? The company's to make it more efficient? The government's to regulate it? All three?"
  • "Does knowing this change anything about how you personally will use AI?"

Final vote: "Put your hand up if knowing this changes — even slightly — how you think about using AI tools." Don't ask anyone to justify. Just count, and acknowledge the number.

PlenaryThe bill48–50 min

Return to the opening hook. Reveal or remind students of the energy costs. Then ask: "In the time this lesson has run, how many AI queries do you think were processed globally?"

(Rough estimate: millions per minute across all major providers.) "Each one used energy. Each one required cooling. Each one was somewhere in someone's data centre."

This isn't an argument against AI. It's an argument for knowing what you're using — and for the industry, policymakers, and individuals to treat that knowledge seriously.

"A tool isn't clean just because you can't see where the power comes from."
Links forward

Key idea

Every time you use AI, a server runs hot somewhere — and knowing that is the beginning of using it responsibly.
