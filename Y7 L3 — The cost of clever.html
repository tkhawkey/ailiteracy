Year 7 Lesson 3 45 min AI & the world

The cost of clever

Students find out how much energy and water AI uses, and leave understanding that using AI responsibly starts with knowing what it costs.

Cambridge Digital Literacy objectives
7DW.02Understand the environmental impact of digital systems, including the energy and water consumption of data centres and AI infrastructure.
7SW.04Consider the ethical responsibility of individuals and organisations when using AI tools at scale.
Resources
  • Teacher device + projector, connected to internet
  • Y7 L3 — The cost of clever (slides).pptx — slides to show on the projector
  • Copilot or ChatGPT — for the AI self-reflection demo
  • Y7 L3 — Student resource to print.html — scenario cards and 2×2 grid, print one set per group of 4–5
  • Optional: large paper or mini whiteboards for the 2×2 grid
Rationale
Year 7 students are not using AI themselves, but it is all around them and they will hear a lot about it. Few people have shown them what it costs. The lesson starts with a vote that surprises them: one prompt uses very little energy, but building a model and answering billions of prompts does not. Students then look at the real numbers and decide for themselves which uses of AI are worth the cost.
HookWhich costs more?0–6 min

Give no introduction. Say: "I'm going to give you three pairs. For each pair, vote on which one uses more energy." Students stand for A, sit for B, or stay where they are if they're not sure.

Pair 1: (A) Boiling a kettle once OR (B) Sending one text prompt to an AI?

Pair 2: (A) A ten-minute shower OR (B) Asking an AI to make ten images?

Pair 3: (A) One person's return flight from London to New York OR (B) Training GPT-3, an earlier ChatGPT model, once?

Reveal the answers: Pair 1: the kettle, by hundreds of times. One prompt uses very little energy. Pair 2: the shower, by more than ten times, even with the most energy-hungry image models. Making images does use far more energy than text, though. Pair 3: training GPT-3 produced about as much carbon as 450 people each flying from London to New York and back.

So one prompt is tiny, but building the model is huge, and billions of prompts add up.

"AI is in the news, in search and in apps, even if you aren't using it in school. Let's find out what it costs, and I don't mean money."
TeachWhere does the power go?6–16 min

What is a data centre? A building, or a group of buildings, full of servers: computers that never switch off and answer requests all day, every day. There are thousands of them around the world, and they look like plain warehouses. In 2024 data centres used about 1.5% of all the electricity in the world, and the International Energy Agency expects that to roughly double by 2030.

Two kinds of AI energy cost:

  • Training — building the model from scratch. Done once (or a few times for major versions). Very expensive. Researchers estimate that training GPT-3 produced about 552 tonnes of CO₂, the same as around 450 people each flying from London to New York and back. The companies behind newer models don't publish their figures, but outside estimates are higher.
  • Inference — answering your questions. This happens billions of times a day. One text prompt uses very little: Google says a typical Gemini prompt uses about 0.24 watt-hours, the same as watching TV for nine seconds. Images and video use much more, and the total adds up because there are so many prompts.

Water: Servers get very hot, and many data centres cool them with water. In July 2022, Microsoft's data centres in Iowa took in about 11.5 million gallons (around 43 million litres) of water, roughly 6% of the local district's supply. That was the month before OpenAI says it finished training GPT-4. In places short of water, data centres compete with farms and households.

Where does the power come from? Tech companies are investing in renewable energy, but demand is growing faster than clean supply in many places. At busy times a data centre often draws power from fossil fuels. Some researchers question the "carbon-neutral" claims of big AI companies.

Teacher note

These numbers are estimates. They change with the model, the hardware and the power source, and some come from the companies themselves. Sources: Patterson et al. (2021) for GPT-3 training; Google (August 2025) for the prompt figure; the International Energy Agency (2025) for data centres; AP and West Des Moines Water Works (2023) for the Iowa water. What matters is the scale, not the exact figure.

Live AI DemoAsk the AI about itself16–22 min

Open Copilot. Ask it two questions in sequence:

Question 1: "How much energy and water does it take to train a large language model like yourself, and what's the environmental impact?"

Let it answer. It should give a fairly honest account, though it may not have exact figures.

Question 2: "Is it your responsibility — or the company that built you — to tell users about this environmental cost? Or is it the user's responsibility to find out?"

This second question has no right answer. The point is to hear the arguments. Take 3–4 student responses.

"Who should be responsible for telling people this? The AI? The company? Governments? Nobody?"

Teacher note

AI tools will usually talk about their own environmental footprint when asked. Point out that the tool answering the question is part of the problem it is describing.

ActivityWorth the cost?22–36 min

Give each group a set of the scenario cards. Each card lists six uses of AI with a rough energy cost: Low, Medium, High or Very high.

The six scenarios:

  • Asking an AI a factual question (one query) — Low
  • Generating a set of ten AI images — Medium
  • Using AI to translate a 10-page document — Low
  • Training a new AI model from scratch for a company — Very high
  • Running a personalised AI tutor for one student, daily, for a full school year — Low–Medium
  • An AI system adjusting a city's traffic lights, 24/7 — Medium

Groups place each scenario on a 2×2 grid:

  • Across: energy cost (low to high)
  • Up: value to people or education (low to high)

The hardest square is top right: high cost, high value. Groups discuss: "Is this worth it? Who should decide? What does 'worth it' mean?"

Each group shares the placement they argued about most. Steer them to the traffic lights and the AI tutor, which cause the most disagreement.

Teacher note

There are no correct placements, and the energy costs on the cards are rough guides, not measurements. Ask groups to explain their reasoning rather than defend a position. The aim is for students to notice that these choices have a cost they usually can't see.

DiscussionWho should decide?36–42 min

Whole-class discussion. Ask the three questions below, about a minute and a half each, then take the vote:

  • "Should AI products carry a carbon label, the way food carries calorie information? Why or why not?"
  • "Is it the user's responsibility to use AI less? The company's to make it more efficient? The government's to regulate it? All three?"
  • "Does knowing this change anything about how you think about AI?"

Final vote: "Put your hand up if knowing this changes, even a little, how you think about AI." Don't ask anyone to explain. Count the hands and say the number out loud.

PlenaryThe bill42–45 min

Go back to the opening vote and remind students of the answers. Then ask: "In the time this lesson has run, how many AI prompts do you think were sent around the world?"

(Nobody knows exactly, but it is many billions a day, so millions in the time this lesson has taken.) "Each one used energy. Each one needed cooling. Each one ran in a data centre somewhere."

This is not an argument against AI. It is a reason to know what you are using.

"A tool isn't clean just because you can't see where its power comes from."
Links forward

Connects to Y8 L4 (Your name on it): if you choose to use a tool that has an environmental cost, that choice is part of using it responsibly.

Key idea

Every time anyone uses AI, a computer somewhere uses power and water, and knowing that is the first step to using it responsibly.
