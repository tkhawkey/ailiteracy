Year 8 Lesson 3 45 min Prompting & tools

Engineer the prompt

Students learn the five parts of a good prompt by fixing weak prompts on paper, and leave able to rewrite a vague prompt so it gets a useful answer.

Cambridge Digital Literacy objectives
8TC.08Explore using general purpose prompting frameworks iteratively, including deciding which parts of each framework structure to adapt.
8SW.07Understand that, as users, they have responsibility for independent thinking along with balanced, thoughtful AI use to protect their wellbeing.

Rationale
Next year students start using AI themselves, so this lesson teaches the skill before they have the tool. They look at three real chats where a one-line prompt got generic work, learn five parts of a good prompt (building on Role, Task and Detail from Year 5), and practise fixing weak prompts on paper. Working without a tool means the skill belongs to them, not to one app.
HookThree one-line prompts0–8 min

Display the three Copilot chats one after another, about 30 seconds each (links in Resources). Don't comment yet. Say: "Each of these students typed one short line. Look at what they typed, then at what came back."

Ask: "What do the three prompts have in common? What did the AI have to guess?" Take 3–4 answers. Students should get to: the prompts are short, and they don't say who the student is or what they need, so the AI guessed and gave something generic.

Then ask about the geography conclusion: "Whose thinking is in this? Could the student explain it if I asked them?"

"The AI filled every gap with a guess. Today we learn how to fill those gaps ourselves."
TeachThe five parts of a prompt8–18 min

Say: "In Year 5 you learned Role, Task and Detail. Today Detail splits into three, which gives five parts." Put these on the board:

  • Role — who the AI should act as. "Act as my science teacher."
  • Task — exactly what you want it to do. "Find the spelling mistakes in my paragraph."
  • Context — what it needs to know about you and the work. "I'm in Year 8 and this is for a science test."
  • Format — how the answer should look. "A short list."
  • Constraints — what to avoid or limit. "Don't rewrite it. Under 100 words."

Go back to the photosynthesis chat on the projector. Ask which of the five parts are missing. Then show this rewrite and ask students to find all five parts in it:

"Act as my Year 8 science teacher. Here is my paragraph about photosynthesis. Don't rewrite it. Point out my spelling mistakes and tell me one scientific idea I've left out. Use a short list."

Ask: "Before we see what the AI would do, what do you predict it will give back?" (A list of corrections, not a new paragraph. The student still has to do the fixing.)

Teacher note

Context and Format can overlap with Task, and students may argue about which label a phrase belongs to. That is fine. What matters is that all five are covered, not what each phrase is called.

ActivityPrompt Surgery18–36 min

Give out the Prompt Surgery sheet. Students work in pairs. Each of the four cases shows a weak prompt and the disappointing answer it got. For each case students:

  • Diagnose: which of the five parts are missing?
  • Rewrite: write a better prompt using the five parts.
  • Predict: what will the new answer look like?

Aim for cases 1 and 2 in the first 10 minutes. Then swap with another pair for 4 minutes: where do your rewrites differ, and whose is stronger? Cases 3 and 4 are for pairs who finish early, or for homework.

What to look for

Case 1 (World War One): missing Context (age, what the homework is), Format and Constraints (length, plain language). Example: "I'm in Year 8 and my homework is to explain the causes of World War One. Give me the four main causes in about 150 words, in plain language, one sentence each."
Case 2 (story ideas): missing Context, a clearer Task and Constraints. Example: "I'm writing a short story for English about a girl who finds a door in school that wasn't there yesterday. Don't write it for me. Ask me five questions that help me decide what happens next."
Case 3 (my argument): missing Role, Task and Constraints. The AI just agreed. Example: "Act as a tough debate judge. My argument is that social media is bad for teenagers. Give me the three weakest points in it. Don't just agree with me."
Case 4 (revision plan): missing Context, Format and Constraints. Example: "I'm in Year 8 with exams in three weeks in maths, science and English. I have 45 minutes each weekday evening and maths is my hardest. Make me a one-week timetable as a table and tell me which topics to start with."

Teacher note

Strong rewrites are specific, not just longer. If a student has only added words, ask: "Does this tell the AI something it didn't know before?"

PlenaryThe precision principle36–45 min

Take three rewrites from volunteers, from case 1 or 2. For each, ask the class: does every word do some work?

Make two points. First, longer is not better: a short prompt with the right information beats a long one that buries the instruction. Second, a prompt also decides who does the thinking. "Write my conclusion" hands the thinking to the AI. "Ask me three questions about my conclusion" keeps it with you.

"Next year you'll be allowed to use AI. The students in those three chats got generic work because they gave it nothing to go on. You now know five things to tell it, and you know how to ask it to help you think instead of thinking for you."

Key idea

A good prompt tells the AI who to be, what to do, what it needs to know, how to answer and what to avoid, and it leaves the thinking with you.
