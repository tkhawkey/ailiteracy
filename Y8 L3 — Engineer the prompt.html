Year 8 Lesson 3 50 min Prompting & tools

Engineer the prompt

Students analyse, predict, and rewrite prompts using a structured anatomy framework — building transferable technique without touching a tool.

Rationale
Prompt engineering without the interface. By separating the skill from the platform, students build analytical technique that transfers across every AI tool they'll ever use.
HookTwo outputs, no prompts0–10 min

Display two AI-generated responses to the same underlying task (e.g. two different essay plans for a WW1 history question) — but don't show the prompts that generated them. One is thorough and specific; one is generic and shallow.

Ask: "These came from the same AI tool, on the same day. Why are they so different?"

Take answers. When students arrive at 'because the question was different', agree: "The prompt is the lever. Let's understand exactly how to pull it."

Display the three bad prompt example PDFs briefly — the photosynthesis, essay plan, and geography prompts. Ask: "What do these have in common? What are they missing?"

TeachPrompt anatomy10–20 min

Introduce the five components of a well-engineered prompt:

  • Role — who should the AI behave as? ('Act as an expert historian...')
  • Task — what precise action? (not 'write something' — 'write a 200-word outline with three main arguments')
  • Context — what does the AI need to know? (audience, subject, prior knowledge level)
  • Format — how should the output be structured? (bullet points, essay, table, numbered list)
  • Constraints — what should it avoid? (no jargon, no lists, under 150 words)

Apply the framework to one of the bad prompt examples — annotate it live on the projector: "What role is implied? What task? What's missing entirely?"

Show a rewritten version. Ask: "Before seeing the output — what do you predict will be different?"

ActivityPrompt Surgery20–40 min

Distribute the Prompt Surgery sheet. Students work individually or in pairs. The sheet presents four prompt/output pairs — a weak prompt and the disappointing output it generated.

For each pair, students:

  • Diagnose: which anatomy components are missing or weak?
  • Rewrite: produce an improved prompt using the framework.
  • Predict: what will the output look like after the rewrite?

After 15 minutes, compare rewrites with a neighbour. Where do your rewrites differ? Whose is stronger — and why?

Teacher note

Strong rewrites are specific, not just longer. A common error is students adding words without adding precision. Ask: 'Does this tell the AI something it didn't know before?'

PlenaryThe precision principle40–50 min

Take 3–4 rewrites from volunteers. For each: does it pass the precision test — does every word do work?

Address a common misconception: "Longer prompts are not always better. A prompt that contains the right information in the right order will outperform a longer one that buries the key instruction."

"Every professional who uses AI as part of their work thinks about prompts. The difference between a mediocre AI user and a skilled one isn't access to better tools — it's knowing how to use the ones they have. That's what you built today."
