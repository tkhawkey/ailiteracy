Year 6 Lesson 3 50 min Prompting & tools

Sharpen it

Students leave with the CIS principle — the thinking is always yours — and practical experience of prompting AI as an improver rather than a replacer.

Cambridge Digital Literacy objectives
6SW.07Understand that all learners have a personal responsibility for fully understanding the academic outputs that they produce.
6SW.08Understand that digital and AI tools do not provide a shortcut to sustained learning.
6DW.11Understand that co-intelligence involves combining human and artificial intelligence, often by using AI to augment human thought and creativity.
Resources
  • Projector with 3 browser tabs open (Copilot) — or 3 windows side by side
  • Whiteboard for tracking error types during shared writing
Rationale
Introduces the CIS principle by showing the same paragraph improved three different ways. Students practise prompt discipline before they have unsupervised AI access at secondary level.
HookThe bad sentence0–5 min

Write on the board:

"Volcanoes are like big rocky hills that sometimes explode and shoot out hot orange stuff from the top and it goes everywhere."

Ask: "What's wrong with this? What makes it not quite right for Year 6 science writing?" Take answers — students usually identify: vague language, informal tone, weak explanation, repetition.

Don't fix it yet. Leave it on the board.

Live AI DemoThree windows, three approaches5–15 min

Open 3 Copilot tabs or windows. Show them side by side if possible. No commentary — just type and let students watch:

Window 1 (Bad — replacing thinking): Write me an in-depth introductory sentence about volcanoes at a Year 6 level.

Window 2 (Still bad — rewriting thinking): Rewrite this sentence for me so it sounds more scientific: [paste the bad sentence]

Window 3 (Good — sharpening thinking): What is weak about this sentence and how could the language improve? Don't rewrite it. [paste the bad sentence]

Then ask: "Which one made the human do the most thinking? Which one did the thinking for them?" Take answers. Name what's different about Window 3.

TeachThe CIS principle15–20 min

Reveal and read aloud:

"The thinking is always yours. AI is how you sharpen it."

Explain simply: AI should help you improve your ideas — not replace them. Then introduce the defensibility test: "Can you explain and defend every part of your work as your own thinking?"

Ask: which of the three windows would pass this test? Which would fail? Students should immediately identify Window 3 as the only defensible approach.

ActivityMake it deliberately bad20–35 min

Choose a class topic (earthquakes, rivers, the water cycle). Write a shared paragraph together — but deliberately include errors. The teacher tracks them visibly:

  • Vague words: 'stuff', 'things', 'goes everywhere'
  • Informal tone: 'really bad and dangerous'
  • Weak explanation: 'because of plates moving and stuff'
  • Repetition, unclear ideas

Build something like: 'Earthquakes are when the ground shakes a lot and it can be really bad and dangerous and things can fall down and it happens because of plates moving and stuff.'

Now ask: "We're allowed to use AI — but only to sharpen our thinking. What should we ask it?" Guide toward a Window 3 prompt: 'Here is our paragraph. What are the three weakest parts and how could we improve them? Don't rewrite it.'

Paste and show the response. Discuss: "What did AI point out? What do we still have to do?" Then improve the paragraph together — human-led, AI-informed.

ActivityBad prompts — spot and fix35–45 min

Display five bad prompts one at a time. After each, ask: "What's wrong with this? How would you fix it?"

  • ❌ 'Write a better paragraph about earthquakes for me.' — AI does all the thinking.
  • ❌ 'Rewrite this so it sounds good but keep the meaning the same.' — Still not your thinking.
  • ❌ 'Make this better.' — No control, AI might take over.
  • ❌ 'What is the best paragraph I could write about earthquakes?' — Removes decision-making.
  • ❌ 'Give me a perfect example I can use.' — Encourages copying.

Students suggest improved versions for 2–3 of them. Good rewrites usually include: 'What is one thing I should improve…', 'Where is my explanation unclear?', 'What words are too informal?'

PlenaryFinal reflection45–50 min

Ask students to complete one sentence each — aloud or on paper: "AI is for ____. AI is not for ____."

Close with:

"AI helps you spot weaknesses, improve clarity, and think more deeply. AI does not write your work or replace your ideas. If you can explain every decision in your work, and defend it as your own thinking — you've used AI well."
