Your name on it

Students apply the principle 'if you can't explain it, it's not yours' to a ranking exercise, a class discussion, and their own academic practice — leaving with a clear, portable integrity framework.

Rationale
The suite's culminating integrity lesson. Frames academic honesty not as rule-following but as a question of what the student can explain, defend, and claim as their own thinking.
HookInterrogation0–9 min

Display an AI-generated paragraph on a topic students have recently studied. Read it aloud. Then ask:

  • "What does this paragraph claim?"
  • "Can anyone explain why the second sentence follows from the first?"
  • "If this were in your essay and I asked you in person — could you explain it?"

The point isn't to trick anyone — it's to surface that fluent, confident-sounding text can be produced without understanding. Ask: "What's the difference between writing that explains something and writing that sounds like it explains something?"

"Here's the principle we'll be working with today: if you can't explain it in your own words to another person — it's not yours. Not because a rule says so. Because your brain hasn't processed it."
ActivityWork sample ranking9–30 min

Distribute the work samples sheet (three responses to the same question about the invention of the printing press). Students read all three, then rank them:

  • Which is the most likely to be AI-generated?
  • Which shows the most genuine understanding?
  • Which would you be most comfortable putting your name on — and why?

Individual ranking first (5 min), then group discussion (8 min). Groups don't need to agree — the reasoning matters more than the ranking.

Teacher note

Sample A is fluent but generic — good vocabulary, thin argument, no specific detail. Sample B is rougher but specific — a real claim with real evidence. Sample C is clearly AI-assisted, with the distinctive 'well-structured but nothing to say' quality. Most students identify these quickly; push them to articulate why.

DiscussionWhat integrity actually means30–42 min

Take group rankings. Where did groups disagree? Examine the disagreements.

Pose three scenarios and discuss each:

  • Scenario A: A student uses AI to generate an essay, reads it, and submits it unchanged. Is this a problem if the essay is accurate?
  • Scenario B: A student drafts an essay, uses AI to improve the language, edits the AI's changes, and submits. Is this a problem?
  • Scenario C: A student asks AI 'what are the main arguments about X?', uses those ideas as a starting point, and writes their own essay from scratch. Is this a problem?

Push beyond 'the rules say': "In each case — what did the student actually learn? What can they now explain? What would happen if you asked them to defend their work in person?"

Teacher note

Scenario B is where most students get stuck — and it's the right place to get stuck. The question isn't whether AI touched the work; it's whether the student can explain and defend the thinking behind every part of it.

PlenaryWhat is left for humans?42–50 min

Students complete the final section of the sheet: "What is left for you, as the human — that AI cannot do in your work?"

Take 5–6 responses. Common powerful answers: knowing what question to ask, caring about the answer, having something to say, being accountable for being wrong.

"You've spent this year learning how AI works, how to use it well, and when not to. The last lesson is this: your name on something means something. It means you can explain it, you can defend it, and you stand behind it. AI can produce text. Only you can produce your thinking."
Teacher note

If your school has an AI use policy, this is a good moment to connect to it specifically — the principle should match the policy. If the policy is evolving, acknowledge that too: the principle is more durable than any current rule.

What comes next

In Year 9 you'll work with AI as a genuine thinking partner — using it to explore ideas, stress-test arguments, and deepen your own understanding. That relationship only works if you know your own thinking clearly enough to recognise when AI has sharpened it and when it has quietly replaced it. Everything in this lesson is preparation for that. Come back in September ready to lead the collaboration.

Key idea

If you can’t explain what each part of your work means, the work is not yours.
