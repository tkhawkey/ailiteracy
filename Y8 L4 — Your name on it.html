Year 8 Lesson 4 45 min Integrity & learning

Your name on it

Students learn a simple test for whether work is really theirs, "could I explain this?", by ranking three student answers and judging real situations. It is the last lesson of the year, so it also points ahead to using AI in Year 9.

Cambridge Digital Literacy objectives
7TC.08Understand plagiarism, and the need for citation and fair use, including when using AI-generated and web-sourced content.
8SW.06Understand how using AI tools to create education-related outputs can be a barrier to deep learning.
8SW.07Understand that, as users, they have responsibility for independent thinking along with balanced, thoughtful AI use to protect their wellbeing.

Rationale
Students decide for themselves where the line is between using AI and handing over their thinking. They start with a quick test, practise it by ranking three student answers, then apply it to real situations. It is the last lesson of the year and prepares them for Year 9, when they start using AI.
HookInterrogation0–8 min

Display this paragraph, which is typical of an AI answer, and read it aloud. If your class has just studied something else, ask Copilot for a paragraph on that topic and use it instead.

Paragraph to display

The fall of the Roman Empire was a complex process shaped by a convergence of internal and external pressures. Economic instability weakened the state's capacity to respond, while political fragmentation undermined its legitimacy. Ultimately, the decline illustrates the fragile interdependence of institutions, society and economy, and offers enduring lessons for modern civilisations.

Then ask:

  • "What is this paragraph actually claiming?"
  • "Can anyone give me an example of an 'internal pressure'? Or explain why the second sentence follows from the first?"
  • "If this were in your essay and I asked you about it in person, could you explain it?"

The point is not to trick anyone. It is to show that smooth, confident text can be produced with nothing underneath it. Ask: "What is the difference between writing that explains something and writing that only sounds as if it does?"

"Here's the test for today: if you can't explain it in your own words to another person, it isn't yours yet. That's true whether or not there's a rule about it."
ActivityWork sample ranking8–25 min

Give out the sheet. It has three answers to the same question about the printing press. Students read all three, then rank them:

  • Which is most likely to have been written by AI?
  • Which shows the most real understanding?
  • Which would you be most comfortable putting your name on, and why?

Roughly: 3 minutes reading, 4 minutes ranking alone, 7 minutes in groups, 3 minutes sharing back. Groups don't need to agree. The reasoning matters more than the ranking.

What to look for

Sample A is polished and generic. It could be about any invention, and it uses stock phrases such as "democratized knowledge" and "overall consensus". Most likely written by AI.
Sample B is the hardest to call. It has a couple of real details (books copied by hand, the Reformation) but also stock phrases, and it repeats "think for themselves". It could be a student working from memory, or someone who used AI and edited it.
Sample C is informal, gives an opinion and compares the printing press to the internet. It is the most clearly one student's own thinking, though it is short on facts.
There is no single right ranking. The best discussion point is that the best-written answer (A) is not the one that is most yours (C). Accept any ranking that comes with a reason.

DiscussionWhat integrity actually means25–37 min

Take the group rankings. Where did groups disagree, and why?

Then give the class three situations, about 3 minutes each:

  • A: A student gets AI to write an essay, reads it, and hands it in unchanged. Is that a problem if the essay is accurate?
  • B: A student writes a draft, uses AI to improve the language, checks and edits the changes, and hands it in. Is that a problem?
  • C: A student asks AI "what are the main arguments about X?", uses them as a starting point, and writes their own essay from scratch. Is that a problem?

Go past "the rules say". In each case ask: "What did the student learn? What can they now explain? Could they defend it in person?" Use the last 2 minutes to pull the three together.

Teacher note

Most students get stuck on B, and that is the right place to get stuck. The question is not whether AI touched the work. It is whether the student can explain and defend the thinking behind every part of it.

PlenaryWhat is left for you?37–45 min

Students fill in Part 3 of the sheet: "What can only you do in your own schoolwork?" (3 minutes). Take 4–5 answers. Link it back to the jobs lesson: "In Lesson 2 you looked for the human advantage at work. This is the same question for your own schoolwork."

Strong answers are usually about knowing what question to ask, caring about the answer, having something to say, and being the one who answers for it if it is wrong.

Finish with the last box on the sheet: "One thing I will do differently in my work this year." (1–2 minutes)

"This year you've learned how AI works, how to use it well, and when not to. Your name on something means you can explain it, defend it, and stand behind it. AI can write the words. You are the one who has to understand them and answer for them."
What comes next

From Year 9 you will use AI in lessons. For each piece of work your teacher will set an AI level, from Level 0 (no AI) up to Level 3 (AI can help you create). Whatever the level, the test is the one from today: could I explain this and stand behind it?

Teacher note

The Year 9 AI levels are: 0 No AI, 1 Think with AI, 2 Draft with AI, 3 Create with AI. Students will meet them properly in Year 9, so this is only a preview. The principle matches the school AI policy.

Key idea

If you can’t explain what each part of your work means, the work is not yours.
