Year 6 Lesson 1 50 min Integrity & learning

What does AI do to your brain?

Students experience the difference between learning with effort and outsourcing to AI — leaving with a felt understanding that the shortcut that saves time costs something real.

Cambridge Digital Literacy objectives
6SW.07Understand that all learners have a personal responsibility for fully understanding the academic outputs that they produce.
6SW.08Understand that digital and AI tools do not provide a shortcut to sustained learning.
6DW.10Explore how AI algorithms can influence human thinking, including the risk that humans offload thinking and decision-making skills to AI.

Rationale
This lesson demonstrates that delegating core thinking to AI hinders genuine learning and skill retention. Through direct comparison, students discover that using AI effectively requires active participation, critical questioning, and intentional engagement—ensuring technology supports their thinking rather than replacing it.
Hook The Pi experiment — Round 1 0–10 min

Before students arrive: divide the room into Group A and Group B. Place one Pi digit strip on each Group B desk — face-up is fine.

Write the first 20 digits of Pi on the board: 3.14159265358979323846. Address each group out loud before starting the timer:

"Group A — your job is to look at the digits on the board and memorise as many as you can. In 90 seconds, I'm covering the board. You'll write what you remember on your mini whiteboard. No strips, no phones — just your brain."
"Group B — you have the number written on your strip. You don't need to memorise anything. In 90 seconds you'll write the digits on your mini whiteboard, and you can use the strip when you do. You can relax - but don't let any of the A group see your strip!"

Start the 90 seconds. Group A will work hard; Group B will sit back. Hopefully, group A will be beavering away to try and memorise the number whilst group B sit back smugly.

Teacher note

If Group A complain it's unfair, acknowledge it rather than reassuring them: "Yes. Remember that feeling." That frustration is part of the payoff — it lands in Round 2.

After 90 seconds, cover the board. Group A writes from memory; Group B copies from their strip. Count up each group's scores and announce them — Group B will of course win.

Collect all strips. Wipe all mini whiteboards. Tell the class: "We'll come back to this."

Teach How memory actually works — Round 2 10–22 min

Play the memory video (approx. 5–7 min). This is to break up the first memory round and the second memory round! Pause once, roughly halfway, to ask: "Does this change your thinking about what Group A and Group B actually did?"

After the video, display Y6 L1 - Major method table.png on screen. Walk through it briefly: each digit 0–9 maps to a consonant sound, and those sounds become words, which become images in a story — a technique that turns abstract numbers into something the brain can hook onto.

Round 2. Ask everyone — including Group B — to write as many Pi digits as they can remember, from memory only. No strips, no notes.

Count up. Group B will typically score far lower than Round 1, often lower than Group A, because they never originally tried to memorise the digits — they just copied them from their strip.

Teacher note — name this to the class

This is the moment to make the analogy explicit. Group B's strip was the AI. When a student pastes a question into AI and copies the response, they are doing exactly what Group B did in Round 1: producing a correct answer quickly, with no effort, and with no learning. When the AI isn't there — in a test, in any moment that requires real thinking — they have nothing to show for it.

Say it to the class: "Group B had the answer written down. Group A had only their brain. Round 1 looks like Group B won. Round 2 tells you who actually learned something. That strip was the AI."

Key message: "Memory forms through retrieval — the effort of trying to recall. If your brain never had to work, nothing stuck. AI can produce your work for you. It cannot do your learning for you. Those are two different things."

Activity The mantis shrimp challenge 22–30 min

Distribute the mantis shrimp reading sheet. Students read silently for 3 minutes. Then sheets are turned face-down.

Students answer the questions on the sheet entirely from memory — no re-reading. 4–5 minutes.

Don't collect sheets yet. Ask: "How did that feel? Was it harder than you expected?"

Live AI Demo AI reads the mantis shrimp 30–35 min

Paste the mantis shrimp text into Copilot on the projected screen. Type: "Answer the following questions about this text:" then paste the questions.

Watch the output appear. Ask the class: "How long did that take? Did the AI have to try?"

Answering the questions using your own memory was like learning the digits of pi. You might not get it all right, but something from what you read has stuck in your brain. Asking the AI to just give you the answer shortcuts that process; you get the right answers, but nothing changes in your brain. You have learned precisely nothing."
Discussion The shortcut costs something 35–42 min

Pose the scenario: Student A spent 10 minutes reading, thinking, and answering imperfectly. Student B spent 2 minutes, copied from AI, got full marks. Who learns more?

Small group discussion (3 mins), then bring back to the class. Key questions:

  • Does the right answer matter if you can't reproduce it yourself?
  • What does your teacher actually want you to get better at?
  • If you use AI for everything now, what happens when it's not available?

Push the harder question: "Is there anything wrong with using AI to help? If so, where's the line?"

Teacher note

Students often split on this. Some argue efficiency matters; some argue the point is learning. Both are valid — the goal is to surface the tension, not resolve it cleanly. The defensibility principle (Year 6 Lesson 3) will formalise this later.

Plenary The Pi competition 42–50 min

Return to Pi. Display the Major method table again. Walk through a worked example: the digit 3 maps to the m sound; 1 maps to t or d; 4 maps to r. So 3.14 could become "MaTe" — an image you can picture. Build the first few digits together as a class, turning them into a short story or set of images.

Give students 3–4 minutes to practise independently. They are trying to encode as many digits of Pi as they can using whatever memory technique works for them — Major System, a story, a song, pure repetition. Let them choose.

"Here's the deal. Over the next few weeks, you're going to have a chance to show how many digits of Pi you've memorised. You could try using the Major Method! We're going to start a Pi Wall: a leaderboard that tracks the record for each person in this class. The only rule is no strips, no phones, no notes. Just your brain."
Teacher note — running the Pi competition

The Pi Wall can be as simple as a display strip on the classroom wall with student names and their current digit count. Students can challenge the record at any point — during form time, at the start of a lesson, or whenever they're ready. Hopefully, this will subtly reinforce the message over the course of a term that active use of your brain is important when using AI!

Close with the callback: "Group B copied the digits. Group A tried to learn them. The Pi Wall only works if you've actually done the hard thing. That's the lesson and it applies to everything, including AI."

Key idea

Letting AI do your thinking saves time, but you only get smarter by doing the hard part yourself.
