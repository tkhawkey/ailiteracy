Year 7 Lesson 1 50 min Critical evaluation

The Algorithm Knows You

Students map their own algorithmic feeds across the different apps they use, examine how personalisation shapes what they see, and practise asking AI questions that get a fuller answer instead of an agreeable one.

Cambridge Digital Literacy objectives
7DW.03Explore how AI algorithms can influence human thinking and decision making, including that rules and quantifiable outcomes might gain prominence over qualitative thinking.
7DW.04Identify positives and negatives of online news, including misinformation and disinformation, and the role that AI can play in these.

Rationale
Students consider how algorithms are used to shape what they see when using an app, in order to keep them engaged and using the service. AI does the same, often taking an agreeable stance to keep you engaging with that particular AI.
HookHands up0–5 min

Hands up: "Does your YouTube, Spotify, or TikTok feed feel like it knows you?" Most hands go up.

Then: "Has it ever shown you something that completely changed your mind about something?" Far fewer hands.

"These apps are using algorithms to give you content that will keep you engaged and using their app. We will look at this, then think about how an AI chatbot does the same thing."
Live AI DemoTwo questions, two answers5–12 min

Note: if you do not want to run the AI live, there is an example of the two outputs in the file "Y7 L1 - Example prompt outputs from Copilot."

Before you start

Open two separate new chats, not one. If you ask both questions in the same conversation, Copilot will remember the first answer and blend its tone into the second, which flattens the contrast you want students to see. Two fresh chats keep the comparison clean.

Chat 1, Prompt A: "Is social media bad for young people?" Read the response aloud.

Chat 2, Prompt B: "Why is social media good for young people?" Read that response aloud.

Ask:

  • What did you notice?
  • Did the AI lie? (No.) Did it tell you the full picture? (Also no.)
  • What was the AI actually doing?

Land the key idea if students don't reach it:

"That's not a glitch. It's how these systems are built, to keep you satisfied and engaged. AI tends to agree with however the question is framed, in the same way a social media feed does."
If the contrast falls flat

Copilot sometimes hedges both ways, giving two similar answers. Don't force it. Say: "Even here, notice it's trying to keep both of us happy rather than pick a side," and move straight into the Feed Map. The point still lands in the Teach phase either way.

ActivityFeed Map12–22 min

Hand out the Feed Map worksheet.

  • 0–5 min: Students work individually, filling in 5 things that regularly appear in each of their feeds (YouTube/video, music, social media).
  • 5–7 min: Students answer the bottom question, "Would your best friend's map look the same?", and pair up to compare their feeds for 2 minutes.
  • 7–10 min: Take 3–4 examples from the room.

The point to draw out: feeds are individual, not shared. The algorithm built something for you specifically.

TeachThe engagement loop22–30 min

Draw on the board as you speak:

"You watch. The algorithm notes it. It shows you more content like it. You watch more. The algorithm becomes more certain."

At each stage: "Who benefits from this loop?" Students usually land on 'the platform'. Confirm: the algorithm isn't trying to show you the truth, it's trying to keep you watching, because that's how the platform makes money.

Connect back to the AI demo: "This is why Prompt A and Prompt B gave such different answers. Both were agreeable. Neither was complete."

Introduce vocabulary:

Filter bubble
A personalised online environment where you mostly see content that matches your existing views and interests, because an algorithm has selected it for you.
Echo chamber
A space, online or offline, where you mainly encounter opinions similar to your own, so they get reinforced rather than challenged.
Engagement metrics
The numbers platforms track to see how much you're using their app, such as likes, watch time, comments, and shares, and use to decide what to show you next.
ActivityBubble Scenario Cards30–42 min

Grouping: create 6 groups.

Each group receives one Bubble Scenario Card, a person whose feed has narrowed around one topic over 6 months. Groups discuss three questions printed on the card:

  • What does this person's feed probably look like now?
  • What are they not seeing anymore?
  • Is that a problem, and if so, for whom?
For your reference (not on the student card)

Most groups land on something like 'the platform' or 'the person themselves' for question 3. If a group is stuck, prompt with "who decided this is what they should see?" rather than giving the answer.

Each group shares their 'missing content' finding (1 min each). Teacher draws out the pattern: every bubble is different, but the structure is the same, more of what you like, less of what challenges you.

PlenaryBetter questions42–50 min

Return to Copilot. Ask: "Knowing what you know now, how would you ask AI a different question to get a fuller answer?"

Take ideas. Students naturally arrive at: ask for both sides, ask it to challenge you, ask 'what are the arguments against this?' Name this: prompt engineering for balance.
